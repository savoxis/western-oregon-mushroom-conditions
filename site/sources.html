Add your own finds

The past finds on the map come from people reporting on two public sites. If you find something, reporting it there helps everyone (including this map), and it stays yours: you choose the license and what location you share.

WhereHowGood to know
iNaturalistFree account, website or phone app. Upload a photo (date and location are usually read from it), and the community suggests or confirms the species.This is where most of the map's finds come from. Only records marked research grade with a Creative Commons license are republished to GBIF.
Mushroom ObserverFree account. Create an observation, then add your photos from its page; other members suggest names. See how to use it.A smaller share of the map's finds. Records are republished to GBIF under a Creative Commons license.

A report appears on this map about one to two weeks after you post it, and only if it has a public location that is reasonably exact. If you hide or blur the location (to protect a favorite spot, for example), it is left off this map, and that is entirely fine. Posting a mushroom you have not identified is welcome on both sites: that is what the community identification is for.

How the scores are made

Scores come from a fruiting-conditions model (soil temperature, moisture, recent rain, frost and season windows for morels, chanterelles and king boletes) run on the weather data above. They estimate conditions, not what is in a basket (see the check against real finds below). This site does not copy any mushroom-hunting forum, map or app, and it does not publish anyone's private spots.

How well do the scores match real finds?

We checked the scores against the public finds on this map (2016 to 2025), counting only days when other mushrooms were being reported nearby so that days with more people out looking did not look better by themselves. The sample is modest, 54 to 377 find-days per mushroom, so treat this as a rough read, not a guarantee. An earlier, smaller check looked better for chanterelles than this larger one does; this is the one to trust.

MushroomWhat we found
ChanterellesAt best a weak match. Reports were about 1.1 to 1.4 times as frequent on prime days (score 60 or more) as on low-score days, and in most of our checks that is within the range of chance.
Fall king boletesThe clearest match: reported about two to three times as often on prime days in most of our checks, from nine zones with enough reports.
Spring king boletesLeans the right way, but too few reports to tell it apart from chance.
Natural morelsNo skill shown. The score covers forest morels at foothill and mountain elevations. More than half of morel reports come from valley floors below about 1,000 feet (parks, yards and orchards), which the score does not cover, so the check is not yet a fair test of the forest scoring.

We found no evidence yet that the scores separate better years from worse years on the same date, and only weak evidence (except for fall kings) that they separate better days from worse days within a season. A modest effect could still be hiding in a sample this size. We repeat the check as more finds and zone history arrive.
