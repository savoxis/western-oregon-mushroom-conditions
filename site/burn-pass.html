What it is not

  • No GPS pins or secret spots. It shows where conditions line up, not where someone found a basket.
  • No promise of mushrooms. Weather, timing and other pickers decide that. The pass will narrow the search; it does not do the walking.
  • Not permission to be anywhere. Greyed-out private land is still private, closures still apply and the map can be wrong. The sign at the gate wins.
  • Not a commercial picking tool. It is built for people filling a basket for dinner.

Before you go

Burned forests kill people. Fire-killed trees fall without warning, most often in wind, and burned roads wash out. Park away from dead trees, stay out of burns on windy days and tell someone where you are going.

  • Most national forests in Oregon require a personal-use permit for morels, usually free, with a daily limit. Selling what you pick needs a commercial permit. Confirm the rules with the ranger district before you go.
  • Fire areas are often closed the first spring, and walking into a closed area can get you cited.
  • Never eat a mushroom you have not identified with certainty. False morels grow in the same places and can kill. Cut every morel in half: a true morel is hollow from tip to stem. Always cook morels; they make people sick raw.

Where the data comes from

Fire outlines from NIFC WFIGS interagency wildfire perimeters, and closures from Forest Service closure orders. For the pass, burn severity will be computed from Sentinel-2 (Copernicus) and Landsat (USGS) imagery, forest type taken from LANDFIRE, land ownership from USGS PAD-US and BLM, and weather from Open-Meteo and NRCS SNOTEL. All public data; see data sources and credits. Research behind the approach: Pilz and others (2004), on morel crops in burned and unburned forests of northeastern Oregon, and Larson and others (2016), on how morels cluster after fire.
